## 2. The site text

This is the actual prose to appear on the page. The target length is 1,200–1,500 words. Each section is one tight block of literary prose, with the facts inside the sentences rather than tacked on.

### Hero

> **A capital learning to look west.**
> *Une capitale qui apprend à regarder vers l'Ouest.*
>
> Boulevards, concert halls, and façades made a public claim: Romanian ambition belonged to a European city, not to a provincial edge.

### A wooden city

Until the 1820s, Bucharest was a wooden city. Painted houses stood behind painted fences, lining muddy streets, with a skyline of Orthodox domes and the ruins of the Old Princely Court at its centre. The eye trained in Paris or Vienna found no single monumental face to read. What the city had instead was a regional style that took its name from a prince.

**Brâncovenesc** decoration — named for **Constantin Brâncoveanu**, Prince of Wallachia from 1688 to 1714 — covered the monasteries and palaces of his age in carved vine, twisted stone columns, and porches that looked like the carven prows of Byzantine ships. The 1724 **Stavropoleos Church** in the old centre is still the textbook example. The Phanariot Greek princes who followed (1711–1821) added mansions with heavy Ionic porticoes in the French classical manner, the future style of the city's first real palaces.

Then came the 1829 **Treaty of Adrianople**, which opened the principalities to European trade and travel. Within a generation, Bucharest was being rebuilt by foreign architects — and the argument about what the city should look like had begun.

*Image caption: Stavropoleos Church, Bucharest — Brâncovenesc porch, 1724. The carved vine and twisted columns of the late-Byzantine-Balkan style that the later city was built on top of.*

### The first foreign architects

The first wave was small, French-led, and political. The Wallachian princes invited architects from Paris and Vienna to design their new palaces, on the logic that a public building is a piece of state furniture.

In 1835 the prince **Alexandru Ghica** commissioned the **Știrbei Palace** on the city's main road from the French architect **Michel Sanjouand** — neoclassical, with a long horizontal façade and a courtyard behind. The same years brought the heavier **Suțu Palace** (1833–1835) on what is now Bulevardul Brătianu, designed by the Austrian architects **Conrad Schwink** and **Johann Veit**, in the late-Romantic Vienna manner. The chief architect of the capital in the 1840s and early 1850s was the Frenchman **Xavier Villacrosse**, who gave his name to the glazed arcade on Strada Lipscani that still stands.

These were still single buildings, set in a city of wooden houses. But the foreign architects were sketching a future: a Bucharest in stone, built in a language that Paris and Vienna would recognise.

*Image caption: Știrbei Palace, Bucharest · Michel Sanjouand · c. 1835. One of the first Western-façade palaces on the old Podul Mogoșoaiei.*

### The age of Carol I

The forty-eight years of **Carol I**'s reign (1866–1914) are the second wave. Carol was a Hohenzollern, brought in from the German Rhineland in 1866 to give the new country a foreign dynasty. His reign is also the period when Bucharest, finally independent after 1877 and 1878, could afford a Western capital.

The country was short of trained architects, so it imported them. The big French and German names of the period — **Albert Galleron**, **Cassien Bernard**, **Paul Gottereau**, **Joseph Hartmann** — worked in Bucharest almost continuously. The Romanian state was the largest single client. It needed a stock exchange, a national bank, an opera house, a concert hall, ministries, schools, hospitals, a royal palace, and a string of public buildings on what was now the city's main avenue.

The dominant style was **Beaux-Arts** or **eclectic** — the French academic manner taught at the **École des Beaux-Arts** in Paris, with its Greek and Roman orders, its mansard roofs, and its elaborate sculptural programmes. In 1878, after the Romanian army's victory at Plevna, the city's old trade road — the *Podul Mogoșoaiei*, the Mogoșoaia Bridge, built by Brâncoveanu in 1692 between his Bucharest residence and his palace at Mogoșoaia — was renamed **Calea Victoriei** in the army's honour. The troops had marched down it on their return. By 1914 the avenue was lined with Beaux-Arts palaces from one end to the other.

*Image caption: Calea Victoriei, Bucharest · c. 1900. The avenue that Brâncoveanu had built in 1692, renamed in 1878, and lined with Beaux-Arts palaces by 1914.*

### The Athenaeum, 1888

The building that was asked to say the most is the **Romanian Athenaeum** (*Ateneul Român*), inaugurated on 14/26 February 1888 at the head of Calea Victoriei. It is the work of the French architect **Albert Galleron**, recommended by **Charles Garnier**, the architect of the Paris Opera (1875). Galleron and his partner **Cassien Bernard** had already designed the **National Bank of Romania** (1883–1900) on Strada Lipscani. The Athenaeum is the round, domed, eight-columned building that still stands at the head of the avenue, with an equestrian statue of Carol I in front of it.

The funding was the larger story. The Romanian Athenaeum Society, founded in 1865 by intellectuals including **Constantin Esarcu**, **V. A. Urechia**, and **Nicolae Kretzulescu**, decided to build a temple of culture but could not afford one. The solution was a national lottery with the rhyming slogan: *"Dați un leu pentru Ateneu!"* — "Donate a leu for the Athenaeum." A generation of Romanians, rich and poor, gave what they could. The result was one of the first monuments of modern Romania paid for by a popular subscription rather than a state budget.

Inside, the acoustics were tuned to symphonic music. A 75-by-3-metre fresco by **Costin Petrescu**, painted in the 1930s, runs around the inside of the dome and tells the Romanian story from the Roman conquest of Dacia (AD 106) to the unification of 1918. On **29 December 1919** the building was the site of the assembly that ratified the union of Bessarabia, Transylvania, and Bukovina with the Romanian Old Kingdom. In **2024** it received the **European Heritage Label** from the European Commission.

*Image caption: The Romanian Athenaeum, Bucharest · Albert Galleron, with Romanian assistants · 1886–1889. The eight Ionic columns, the Baroque dome, and the equestrian statue of Carol I in front of the building.*

### A walk down Calea Victoriei

The Athenaeum is one building on a long avenue. The walk from Piața Națiunile Unite north to Piața Victoriei takes in most of the architectural signatures of the period.

The **CEC Palace** (1897–1900), the **House of Deposits, Consignments and Economy**, was designed by the French architect **Paul Gottereau** and built by the Romanian **Ion Socolescu**. A glass-and-metal central dome; an eclectic façade. The site had been a Brâncoveanu monastery and inn. The palace is the only major Bucharest building to have survived every major earthquake essentially intact.

The **Cantacuzino Palace** (1898–1906) was built for **Gheorghe Grigore Cantacuzino**, nicknamed **Nababul** — "the Nabob" — and designed by the Romanian **Ion D. Berindey**. Eclectic, with Art Nouveau touches, a carved limestone façade, and an interior that is now the **George Enescu Museum**. It was the largest private residence in the city: a statement of private wealth in a country that had just acquired national wealth.

Further north, the **Telephone Palace** (1929–1934) is Bucharest's first skyscraper, and a clear change of style. **Art Deco**, not eclectic. Visible from the older buildings on the avenue, it is one of the first signs that the Beaux-Arts moment is ending and the interwar modern is beginning.

*Image caption: The Cantacuzino Palace, Bucharest · Ion D. Berindey · 1898–1906. Today the George Enescu Museum.*

### The Romanian answer

Not every important architect of the period was French. Around 1900, a recognisably Romanian school began to develop. Its leading figure was **Ion Mincu** (1852–1912), a graduate of the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris, who tried to give the academic French form a national inflection: peasant embroidery patterns, Brâncovenesc columns, traditional joinery, the wooden porch of the Bucharest *mahala*. His **Lahovary House** (1886) is the standard example. Mincu died in 1912, just before his school had fully come of age, but his students — **Ion Socolescu**, **Grigore Cerkez**, **Petre Antonescu**, **Marcel Iancu** — carried the work into the interwar period and beyond.

The parallel institution is the **National School of Fine Arts** in Bucharest, founded in **1864** by the painter **Theodor Aman** and the painter **Gheorghe Tattarescu** at the decree of **Alexandru Ioan Cuza**. Aman had returned from Paris in 1857 and spent seven years lobbying for the school. He became its first director, and held the post until his death in 1891. The school is the ancestor of today's **Bucharest National University of Arts** and the institutional centre of the parallel story told on the Art & Poetry annex.

The Romanian inflection — Brâncovenesc ornament, peasant craft, national themes — was the answer to the question the imported style had raised. A young country could acquire a Western horizon without ceasing to be itself.

*Image caption: Lahovary House, Bucharest · Ion Mincu · 1886. A French-trained architect's first attempt to give the academic style a Romanian inflection.*

### A layered city

What is visible in 1914 is not what is visible in 1880. The city had acquired a new face — what the *gazetari* called **mica Paris**, **Micul Paris** — "Little Paris," the nickname that foreign travel books repeated through the 1920s. The face was also vulnerable. The buildings were made by foreign architects of imported stone, paid for by foreign loans, and within a generation two of the three great powers that had made them — the German and the Russian — would be at war with Romania.

The earthquake of **1940** cracked several palaces. The 1944 Allied bombing destroyed the Suțu, the Sturdza, and the old National Theatre. The communist period nationalised and rebuilt. **Nicolae Ceaușescu**'s 1980s project — the **Palace of the Parliament**, 270 metres long, 240,000 square metres, 12 storeys — bulldozed a chunk of the historic centre to make way for a new civic core, including the boulevard that now bears his name. Brâncovenesc monasteries, Belle Époque houses, and Orthodox churches disappeared in the process.

Today a quarter of old Bucharest is gone. What remains is a palimpsest: Brâncovenesc churches in the back streets, Beaux-Arts palaces on the avenues, Art Deco on the side streets, brutalist concrete on the boulevards, and the still-standing Calea Victoriei with the Athenaeum at its head. The argument the 1880s began — that a young country could acquire a Western horizon without becoming an imitation — is still visible in the street, in the seams between the layers, and in the streets that have kept the names Brâncoveanu gave them in 1692.

*Image caption: Calea Victoriei, Bucharest · today. The same avenue Brâncoveanu built in 1692, with the Athenaeum of 1888 at its head and the city's later layers behind it.*

## 3. Editorial guidelines (for the builder)

These rules should govern the build, regardless of which model assembles the page. They are written so they can be lifted verbatim and dropped into a system prompt.

### Voice

- **One voice, not several.** The page is written in one literary register — short, declarative, image-led, French-trained. Do not switch to a guidebook voice for the dates or a brochure voice for the buildings.
- **Name the people, not only the works.** "Albert Galleron, recommended by Charles Garnier" reads better than "designed by the French firm Galleron & Bernard." The reader is being asked to recognise names, not catalogue firms.
- **Let the image do the work.** A single sentence about the Athenaeum is usually enough; the photograph of the building does the rest. The text anchors what the eye sees.
- **No "X is not A. It is B" reframes.** This is the pattern that reads as AI-generated. Use a positive verb instead: "Bucharest was a wooden city" reads better than "Bucharest was not yet a stone city."

### Length

- **Hero deck: 1–2 sentences, 25–40 words.** A promise of the page, not a summary.
- **Section body: 1–5 sentences, 80–180 words.** A single tight paragraph is usually right. If a section needs more, break it into panels.
- **Section headlines: 2–6 words, evocative.** "The age of Carol I" works; "Carol I and the modernisation of Bucharest" does not.
- **Statement line: 1 short sentence.** A single image of the page's argument.

### Image rules

- **One hero image.** Use the existing `calea-victoriei-1900.jpg`. The deck sets the historical period.
- **One image per section, where the section is about a specific building.** The Athenaeum needs a photograph of the Athenaeum; the Cantacuzino Palace needs a photograph of the Cantacuzino Palace.
- **No decorative or generated imagery.** All images must be sourced from Wikimedia Commons, the named museums, or the existing asset folder.
- **Captions are dated, credited, and short.** "Calea Victoriei, Bucharest · c. 1900 · postcard, Wikimedia Commons."

### French echo

- **One French line per headline, italic, restrained.** It is a translation of the headline, not of the body. It sits beneath the English headline, not after every paragraph.
- **No French echo for body sentences.** The body is English-first. The French is a resonance, not a duplicate.
- **If a French line is too long to fit, cut it.** The point is to keep the French as a chord, not a harmony.

### Sourcing

- **Every named building has a source in the drawer.** No exceptions.
- **No quotations unless they have a citation.** "Dați un leu pentru Ateneu" is quoted because the slogan is in the historical record. "Little Paris" is paraphrased because it is a nickname, not a quotation.
- **The Bucharest Municipality Museum and ICR are preferred for the human-interest details.** Wikipedia is the entry point, not the final source, for the architectural dates.

### Pitfalls to avoid

- **Do not turn the page into a list of buildings.** A walk down Calea Victoriei is a walk, not an inventory. Three named buildings is the right density.
- **Do not foreground the communist demolition.** The 1940 earthquake, the 1944 bombing, and the 1980s demolition are real and belong in the page, but they should sit at the end, in the "layered city" section, as a single short paragraph. The page's centre of gravity is the 1888–1914 moment, not the loss.
- **Do not introduce a "Best of Bucharest" framing.** The page is about a moment, not a destination guide.
- **Do not use modern street names for the old period.** Brâncoveanu's road is the *Podul Mogoșoaiei* until 1878. The Curtea Veche is the Old Princely Court, not a museum.
- **Do not name the architect's firm; name the architect.** A French-trained reader recognises Garnier's name. They do not recognise a firm.
- **Do not write a "before and after" composition.** The "wooden city → stone city" arc is the page's argument, but it should be carried by the prose, not by parallel headings.

## 4. My thinking (for the author only)

The page is organised around a single argumentative spine: **a wooden city became a stone city, and the stone city kept the wooden city's name**. Every section is one step on that spine.

- **Why start with the wooden city?** Because the Beaux-Arts Bucharest of 1914 cannot be read as a self-contained object. The Brâncovenesc, the Phanariot mansions, the monasteries are the ground the new city was laid on. Cutting the opening section would make the page read as a story of import, not a story of construction.
- **Why name Carol I so early?** Because he is the political container of the second wave. Without him, the imported architects have no commissions. He is also the answer to the "where is the country in this story?" question.
- **Why give the Athenaeum its own large panel?** Because the Athenaeum is the only building in the city that was paid for by a popular subscription, designed on Garnier's recommendation, and used to ratify the union in 1919. It is the building that contains the whole argument. A small panel would underplay it.
- **Why only three buildings in the Calea Victoriei walk?** Because a walk that names six buildings is a list, and a walk that names two is too short to feel like a walk. Three is the density at which a Western reader can picture the avenue.
- **Why a separate section for the Romanian school?** Because the page's central question is whether the imported style erased what was there before. The Brâncovenesc-Romanian school is the answer, and the answer needs its own visible space.
- **Why end with the layered city, not the Beaux-Arts moment?** Because the page is not a 1914 postcard. It is a 2026 view that recognises 1914 inside it. The Athenaeum is still there. The Palace of the Parliament is now there. The page that ends in 1914 will, in five years, read as a period piece. The page that ends in the layered city will still read as a description of the place.
- **What I am not sure about.** I am not sure whether to keep the "first foreign architects" section as its own section, or fold it into the "age of Carol I" section. The 1833–1850 architects matter historically, but on the page they may slow the rhythm. My instinct is to keep them as a small section — a single paragraph and one image — so the reader sees that the imported style is not a sudden 1880s event. A second pass may remove it.
- **What I am deliberately omitting.** The Palace of the Parliament does not get its own panel. It is the most photographed building in Bucharest and the most politically loaded, and putting it on the same footing as the Athenaeum would distort the page's argument. It belongs in the closing paragraph, where the loss is acknowledged in a single sentence.
