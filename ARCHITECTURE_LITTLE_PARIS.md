## A capital without a profile

In the 1820s Bucharest is still an Ottoman-Balkan city: wooden houses, painted in green and blue, set behind wooden fences, lining muddy streets. The skyline is the silhouette of churches: the **Old Princely Court** with its **Curtea Veche** chapel, **Stavropoleos**, **Colțea**, the monastic complexes along the Dâmbovița. Between them, low houses with deep porches and overhanging eaves. The city's craft is in the wood and the plaster, not the stone. The eye, the European eye trained in Paris or Vienna, finds no single monumental face to read.

What the city has, instead, is a set of regional styles, layered. The 17th- and 18th-century **Brâncovenesc** decoration — named for Prince Constantin Brâncoveanu (r. 1688–1714) — covers monasteries, palaces, and churches in a distinctive mix of late Byzantine column, Ottoman arabesque, and Renaissance ornament: thick columns wrapped in vine, balconies of twisted stone, and porch roofs that look like the carven prows of Byzantine ships. The **Hurezi Monastery** (1690s, Oltenia), the **Stavropoleos Church** (1724, Bucharest), and the **Mogoșoaia Palace** (1702, on the road north) are its high points. The Phanariot Greek princes (1711–1821) add mansions with heavy Ionic porticoes in the French classical manner, the future style of the city's first real palaces.

The 1829 Treaty of Adrianople opens the principalities to European trade and travel. Within a generation Bucharest is being rebuilt by foreign and Romanian architects, and the city starts to look like an argument rather than a cluster.

## The first Western architects

The first wave is small and French-led. The Wallachian princes invite architects from Paris and Vienna to design their new palaces, on the logic that a public building is a piece of state furniture. In 1835 the Wallachian prince **Alexandru Ghica** commissions the **Știrbei Palace** on the city's main road from the French architect **Michel Sanjouand** — neoclassical, with a long horizontal façade and a courtyard behind. The same years bring the **Suțu Palace** (1833–1835, on what is now Bulevardul Brătianu), designed by the Austrian architects **Conrad Schwink** and **Johann Veit**: a tall, heavy house with neoclassical orders and Gothic-revival window frames, in the late-Romantic Vienna manner.

The chief architect of the capital in the 1840s and early 1850s is **Xavier Villacrosse**, a Frenchman who gives his name to the **Pasajul Villacrosse**, the glazed arcade on Strada Lipscani that still stands. He is followed by other French and Italian names. Bucharest is becoming, for the first time, a city of architects rather than only of master masons.

These are still single buildings. The street itself is unchanged: an old trade road called the **Podul Mogoșoaiei** (the Mogoșoaia Bridge), named for the road that Brâncoveanu had built in 1692 between his Bucharest residence and his palace at Mogoșoaia. The road is renamed **Calea Victoriei** in 1878, after the Romanian army's victory at Plevna and the triumphant entry of the troops down this very avenue. By the time of the renaming, the avenue has the bones of what it is to become.

## The age of Carol I (1866–1914)

The forty-eight years of **Carol I**'s reign are the second wave. Carol is a Hohenzollern, brought in from the German Rhineland in 1866 to give the new country a foreign dynasty. His reign is also the period when Bucharest, finally independent after 1877 and 1878, can afford a Western capital.

The country is short of trained architects, so it imports them. The big French and German names of the period work in Bucharest almost continuously between 1880 and 1914. The Romanian state is the largest single client. The new state needs a stock exchange, a national bank, an opera house, a concert hall, ministries, schools, hospitals, a royal palace, and a string of public buildings on Calea Victoriei and around it. Architects arrive from Paris, Vienna, and Berlin. Romanian students go the other way.

The dominant style is what the period calls **Beaux-Arts** or **eclectic** — a French academic manner that mixes Greek and Roman orders with Renaissance ornament, mansard roofs, and elaborate sculptural programmes. It is the official style of the **École des Beaux-Arts** in Paris, the training ground of most of the foreign architects now working in Bucharest. The result, in a couple of decades, is a city of about 350,000 inhabitants (1914) lined with buildings that look, in their broadest outlines, like the central districts of Lyon, Turin, or Brussels — but for the churches, the synagogues, the Orthodox domes, and the small wooden houses that still cluster in the side streets.

## The Romanian Athenaeum

The building that is asked to say the most is the **Romanian Athenaeum** (*Ateneul Român*), inaugurated on 14/26 February 1888 on Calea Victoriei. It is the work of the French architect **Albert Galleron**, recommended by **Charles Garnier**, the architect of the Paris Opera (1875). Galleron and his partner **Cassien Bernard** have already designed the **National Bank of Romania** (1883–1900) on Strada Lipscani. The Athenaeum is the round, domed, eight-columned building that still stands at the head of the avenue.

The funding is the larger story. The Romanian Athenaeum Society, founded in 1865 by intellectuals and scientists including **Constantin Esarcu**, **V. A. Urechia**, and **Nicolae Kretzulescu**, decides to build a temple of culture but cannot afford one. The solution is a national lottery with the rhyming slogan: *"Dați un leu pentru Ateneu!"* — "Donate a leu for the Athenaeum." A generation of Romanians, rich and poor, give what they can. The result is one of the first monuments of modern Romania paid for by a popular subscription rather than a state budget.

The building is a domed circle — appropriate, since the foundations were originally laid for a riding school. Inside, the acoustics are tuned to symphonic music. A 75-by-3-metre fresco by **Costin Petrescu**, painted in the 1930s, runs around the inside of the dome and tells the Romanian story from the Roman conquest of Dacia (AD 106) to the unification of 1918. On 29 December 1919 the building is the site of the assembly that ratifies the union of Bessarabia, Transylvania, and Bukovina with the Romanian Old Kingdom. In 2024 it is awarded the **European Heritage Label** by the European Commission.

## A walk down Calea Victoriei

The Athenaeum is one building on a long avenue. The walk from Piața Națiunile Unite north to Piața Victoriei takes in most of the architectural signatures of the period.

- **CEC Palace** (1897–1900) — the **House of Deposits, Consignments and Economy** — designed by the French architect **Paul Gottereau** and built by the Romanian **Ion Socolescu**. A glass-and-metal central dome; an eclectic façade. The site was a Brâncoveanu monastery and inn. The palace is the only major Bucharest building to have survived every major earthquake essentially intact.
- **The National Museum of Art** (former Royal Palace, rebuilt 1930s; original palace from 1837) — the official residence of the royal family after Carol I's arrival, rebuilt in neoclassical style by the French architect **Paul Gottereau** in the 1930s after a fire.
- **The Cantacuzino Palace** (1898–1906) — built for **Gheorghe Grigore Cantacuzino**, nicknamed **Nababul** ("the Nabob"), designed by the Romanian architect **Ion D. Berindey**. Eclectic, with Art Nouveau touches, a carved limestone façade, and an interior that is now the **George Enescu Museum**. The palace, then the largest private residence in the city, is meant to be read as a statement of private wealth in a country that has just acquired national wealth.
- **The Telephone Palace** (1929–1934) — Bucharest's first skyscraper, and a clear change of style. **Art Deco**, not eclectic. Visible from the older buildings on Calea Victoriei, it is one of the first signs that the Beaux-Arts moment is ending and the interwar modern is beginning.
- **The Știrbei Palace** (c. 1835, Michel Sanjouand; with a third storey and caryatids added in 1882 by **Joseph Hartmann**) — the oldest of the great houses, a reminder that the Western façade is not new in 1888 but a generation old.

The buildings are a portrait of the ruling class: boyar and Phanariot fortunes married to French architects and Romanian builders, then to a German dynasty, and finally to a native bourgeoisie of bankers, diplomats, and landowners.

## The Romanian school of architecture

Not every important architect of the period is French. The country's first school of architecture is the **National School of Fine Arts** in Bucharest, founded in **1864** by the painter **Theodor Aman** and the painter **Gheorghe Tattarescu** at the decree of Prince Alexandru Ioan Cuza. Aman, who has returned from Paris in 1857, becomes the school's first director; Tattarescu has studied in Rome. The school is the ancestor of today's **Bucharest National University of Arts**.

Around 1900 a recognisably Romanian school begins to develop. Its leading figure is **Ion Mincu** (1852–1912), a graduate of the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris, who tries to give the academic French form a national inflection: peasant embroidery patterns, Brâncovenesc columns, traditional joinery, the wooden porch of the Bucharest *mahala* (suburb). His **Lahovary House** (1886) and his **Pensions of the Ministers** project are the standard examples. Mincu dies in 1912, just before his school has fully come of age, but his students — **Ion Socolescu**, **Grigore Cerkez**, **Petre Antonescu**, **Marcel Iancu** — carry the work into the interwar period and beyond.

This Romanian inflection is one of the answers to the question the city is asking: how to be Western without ceasing to be Romanian. Bucharest's answer, between 1880 and 1914, is to import the form and to argue about the detail.

## A layered city

What is visible in 1914 is not what is visible in 1880. The city has acquired a new face — and a new vulnerability. The face is what the *gazetari* call **mica Paris** or **Micul Paris** ("Little Paris"), the nickname that foreign travel books repeat through the 1920s. The vulnerability is that the buildings are made by foreign architects of imported stone, paid for by foreign loans, and that within a generation two of the three great powers that have made them — the German and the Russian — will be at war with Romania.

The earthquake of **1940** cracks several palaces. The **Second World War** damages others; the 1944 Allied bombing destroys the Suțu Palace, the Sturdza Palace, the former National Theatre. The communist period after 1947 nationalises and rebuilds. **Nicolae Ceaușescu**'s 1980s project — the **Palace of the Parliament** (then the *Casa Poporului*, "House of the People"), 270 metres long, 240,000 square metres, 12 storeys — bulldozes a chunk of the historic centre to make way for a new civic core, including the boulevard that now bears his name. Brâncovenesc monasteries, Belle Époque houses, and Orthodox churches disappear in the process. Today a quarter of old Bucharest is gone, and what remains is a palimpsest: Brâncovenesc churches in the back streets, Beaux-Arts palaces on the avenues, Art Deco on the side streets, brutalist concrete on the boulevards, and the still-standing Calea Victoriei, with the Athenaeum at its head.

The argument the 1880s began — that a young country could acquire a Western horizon without becoming an imitation — is still visible in the street, in the seams between the layers, and in the streets that have kept the names Brâncoveanu gave them in 1692.

## A small calendar

- **1688–1714** — Reign of Constantin Brâncoveanu; the Brâncovenesc style and the Mogoșoaia road (later Calea Victoriei).
- **1724** — Stavropoleos Church built in Bucharest, in Brâncovenesc style.
- **1692** — Brâncoveanu builds the road that becomes Calea Victoriei; 1702 the Mogoșoaia Palace.
- **1829** — Treaty of Adrianople; principalities opened to European trade and travel.
- **1835** — Știrbei Palace, designed by Michel Sanjouand.
- **1833–1835** — Suțu Palace, by Conrad Schwink and Johann Veit.
- **1840s–1850s** — Xavier Villacrosse, chief architect of Bucharest.
- **1864** — Theodor Aman and Gheorghe Tattarescu found the National School of Fine Arts.
- **1866–1914** — Reign of Carol I.
- **1878** — Podul Mogoșoaiei renamed Calea Victoriei; Romanian independence.
- **1883–1900** — National Bank of Romania, by Cassien Bernard and Albert Galleron.
- **1886** — Lahovary House, by Ion Mincu.
- **1886–1889** — Romanian Athenaeum built, by Albert Galleron.
- **1895** — Society of Romanian Architects founded, in Bucharest.
- **1897–1900** — CEC Palace, by Paul Gottereau and Ion Socolescu.
- **1898–1906** — Cantacuzino Palace, by Ion D. Berindey.
- **1911** — Military Circle (Cercul Militar Național), by Dimitrie Maimarolu.
- **1929–1934** — Telephone Palace, Art Deco.
- **1940** — Major earthquake; many buildings damaged.
- **1944** — Allied bombing; further loss.
- **1984–1997** — Palace of the Parliament built; a quarter of old Bucharest is demolished.

## A small atlas of names

- **Constantin Brâncoveanu** — Prince of Wallachia 1688–1714; the road, the Mogoșoaia Palace, the Hurezi Monastery, and the Brâncovenesc style.
- **Xavier Villacrosse** — French architect, chief architect of Bucharest in the 1840s–1850s; the Villacrosse passage bears his name.
- **Michel Sanjouand** — French architect, designer of the Știrbei Palace (c. 1835).
- **Conrad Schwink and Johann Veit** — Austrian architects, designers of the Suțu Palace (1833–1835).
- **Albert Galleron** — French architect, designer of the Romanian Athenaeum (1886–1889) and partner on the National Bank of Romania. Recommended by Charles Garnier.
- **Charles Garnier** — architect of the Paris Opera; the recommendation that brought Galleron to Bucharest.
- **Cassien Bernard** — French architect; partner of Galleron on the National Bank.
- **Paul Gottereau** — French architect; CEC Palace (project) and the National Museum of Art (later rebuilding).
- **Ion D. Berindey** — Romanian architect; the Cantacuzino Palace (1898–1906) and the Oteteleșanu House.
- **Ion Mincu** (1852–1912) — Romanian architect, École des Beaux-Arts graduate; founder of a national school of architecture.
- **Ion Socolescu** — Romanian architect, builder of the CEC Palace and a bridge between the eclectic and the interwar modern.
- **Theodor Aman** (1831–1891) — Romanian painter, founder of the National School of Fine Arts (1864) and first director.
- **Carol I of Romania** — Hohenzollern prince, first king of independent Romania; the Calea Victoriei is renamed for his army in 1878.
- **Gheorghe Grigore Cantacuzino ("Nababul")** — the "Nabob" of Bucharest; the Cantacuzino Palace is his commission.
- **Nicolae Ceaușescu** — the communist leader whose 1980s demolition project destroyed a quarter of the historic centre.
