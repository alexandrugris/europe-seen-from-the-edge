## 2. The site text

This is the actual prose to appear on the page. The target length is 1,400–1,800 words. Each section is one tight block of literary prose, with the facts inside the sentences rather than tacked on.

### Hero

> **A people learns to speak in public.**
> *Un peuple apprend à parler en public.*
>
> A modern country needs more than a border and a law. It needs images, poems, stages, and arguments in which its people can recognise themselves.

### Before the public

Until the 1820s the Romanian principalities had almost no public art and no public stage. Icons were painted in monasteries in the post-Byzantine tradition. Secular painting was a court craft. Theatre was in Greek — the language of the Phanariot princes — and was staged for boyar families in private rooms.

Two events changed this. The first was the **end of Phanariot rule in 1821**, after Tudor Vladimirescu's uprising in Wallachia and the Greek revolution that followed. The principalities fell back to native boyar rule, and the local language began to claim the public spaces. The second was the **1829 Treaty of Adrianople**, which opened the principalities to European trade and travel.

Within ten years, the first Romanian-language newspaper (*Curierul Românesc*, edited by Ion Heliade Rădulescu, 1829), the first Romanian-language school (*Gheorghe Lazăr* in Bucharest, 1818), and the first Romanian theatre (the *Theatre by the Red Drinking Fountain*, founded at the request of Ralu Caragea in 1817) were in place. The infrastructure of a public culture had begun. Almost every artist and writer of the next generation would have to be sent abroad, and almost all of them would pass through Paris.

*Image caption: A page from the Curierul Românesc, the first Romanian-language periodical, founded by Ion Heliade Rădulescu in 1829.*

### Rosenthal, 1850

The **1848 revolution** in Wallachia is the political event that gives a generation of Romanian artists their first public subject. Among them is the painter **Constantin Daniel Rosenthal** (1820–1851), a Hungarian-born Transylvanian who had studied in Munich and Paris and had been living in Bucharest since 1847. He was at the side of the Rosetti family during the revolutionary days. After the revolution was crushed, he walked into exile in Paris.

In Paris, in 1850, Rosenthal painted the small oil that is now the most reproduced image of the 1848 generation: ***România revoluționară*** — *Revolutionary Romania*. A woman in Romanian folk dress, bust-length, slightly turned to the left, holds the tricolour in her left hand and a dagger in her right. The model is **Maria Rosetti** (née Mary Grant, a Scot), the wife of the revolutionary **C. A. Rosetti** and one of the few women present at the barricades. The painter was working from Delacroix — *Liberty Leading the People* and *Greece Expiring on the Ruins of Missolonghi* — and the painting's allegorical structure is the standard European Romantic one: an abstract political idea made into a female body, the nation's face as the face of a woman.

The painting was lithographed and circulated as propaganda among the Romanian exile community. In 1857 it was donated, with a companion piece, to the museum in Bucharest. Both paintings are now in the **National Museum of Art of Romania**. Rosenthal died of tuberculosis in Paris in 1851, at thirty-one. He was twenty years too early to see Romanian independence.

*Image caption: Constantin Daniel Rosenthal, România revoluționară, 1850. Oil on canvas, 78.5 × 63.5 cm. National Museum of Art of Romania, Bucharest.*

### Aman and the school, 1864

The painter who gave Romania its first generation of trained artists was **Theodor Aman** (1831–1891). Aman studied in Paris in the 1850s, returned to Bucharest in 1857, and spent the next seven years lobbying for a national art school. In **1864**, with his friend and colleague **Gheorghe Tattarescu** (1820–1894, trained in Rome), he persuaded **Prince Alexandru Ioan Cuza** to sign the founding decree. The **National School of Fine Arts** in Bucharest — the ancestor of today's **Bucharest National University of Arts** — opened its doors. Aman was its first director, and he held the post until his death in 1891.

The school was French in its curriculum, German in its discipline, and Romanian in its enrolment. The students — among them **Nicolae Grigorescu**, **Ștefan Luchian**, **Octav Băncilă**, and a generation of younger painters — would go on to define the visual canon of the modern country. None of them would work in a vacuum. Each of them would pass through a foreign capital — Paris for most, Munich for some — and each of them would come back to teach at Aman's school, or to be exhibited at the National Salon that opened in 1864 alongside the school itself.

### Grigorescu and Barbizon

The most important of Aman's students was **Nicolae Grigorescu** (1838–1907), who is usually called the founder of modern Romanian painting. Grigorescu had been trained as an icon painter and had been painting frescoes in churches since childhood. In **1861** the Romanian state sent him to Paris on a scholarship. He entered the **École des Beaux-Arts** and was admitted to the workshop of **Sébastien Cornu**, where his fellow student was **Pierre-Auguste Renoir**. Within two years he had left the École and moved to **Barbizon**, the village south of Paris where the generation of **Théodore Rousseau**, **Jean-François Millet**, **Jean-Baptiste-Camille Corot**, and **Gustave Courbet** had just invented landscape painting in the open air.

Grigorescu stayed in France, on and off, until 1869. He worked the forest of Fontainebleau, painted the village of Marlotte, travelled to Brittany (Vitré, Dinan) and Normandy. In 1867 he exhibited seven paintings at the **Paris Universal Exhibition**, including *Les Lavandières*. In **1868** he was one of a group of Barbizon painters whose work was shown to the imperial couple: **Napoleon III** and **Eugénie** bought his *Apple Blossoms* for the Tuileries collection. He returned to Bucharest in 1869, but kept a Paris studio until 1890.

What he brought back was three things. The first was **plein-air painting** — the practice of working outside, in front of the subject, instead of finishing in the studio. The second was a **modern palette**, built around the broken greens, ochres, and pearly greys of the Barbizon painters, soon to be picked up by the Impressionists. The third was a way of painting **Romanian peasant life** — ox-carts on muddy roads, *ie* (farmhouse) courtyards with chickens, women washing clothes by the river, the same subjects Millet had used for the French peasant — as the legitimate subject of serious painting. The country was no longer the costume. It was the painting. The Barbizon Museum's later curator would call him *"the disciple of the French school."*

*Image caption: Nicolae Grigorescu, Apple Blossoms (Pomii înfloriți), c. 1868. Acquired by Napoleon III and Empress Eugénie for the Tuileries collection.*

### Alecsandri and the folk voice

In poetry, the equivalent of Grigorescu's peasant landscape was the work of **Vasile Alecsandri** (1821–1890): poet, playwright, folklorist, diplomat, minister. Alecsandri was the first major figure of modern Romanian literature.

He published the first major collection of Romanian folk poetry (*Poezii populare ale românilor*) in 1866, including the verse dramas *Despot Vodă* and *Dumbrava Roșie*. He wrote the first Romanian comedy (*Fântâna Blanduziei*, 1843) and the first Romanian historical dramas (*Despot Vodă*, 1879; *Fata de la Cozia*). He collected folk songs, including the version of *Miorița* that became the Romanian national ballad. He was Romania's foreign minister in 1859 and ambassador to Paris from 1880 to 1885.

His position in the Romanian literary canon is the position of a Béranger or a Lamartine in the French: a poet who gave a young language the air of a literature. The folk material Grigorescu was painting, the same material that would feed Eminescu and the next generation, was already collected, edited, and published by Alecsandri in the 1850s and 1860s.

### Eminescu, 1883

The next generation's poet was **Mihai Eminescu** (1850–1889), and his case was different. Eminescu was a Romanian poet who read **Schopenhauer** in German, **Kant** in German, and the **Upanishads** in German; he lived in Vienna (1869–72) and Berlin (1872–74). He was, in temperament, more German than French, and more metaphysical than political. He was not, on the surface, an inheritor of Alecsandri.

In the literary society **Junimea** in Iași — founded in 1863 by **Titu Maiorescu** and friends, with its journal *Convorbiri literare* — Eminescu published the poems that became the core of the modern canon: *Luceafărul* (1883), *Odă în metru antic*, and the five *Scrisori* (Epistles).

*Luceafărul* — variously translated as "The Morning Star," "The Evening Star," "The Lucifer," "The Vesper" — was his long philosophical poem, ten years in the making, published in 1883 from Vienna. It was based on a Romanian folk ballad but reached into Indo-European myth and Schopenhauerian metaphysics. It was about genius and the impossibility of love. The critic **Eugen Lovinescu** called it *"suffused with Schopenhauerian pessimism."* The philosopher **Constantin Noica** read it as a meditation on *"ontological dissatisfaction."* It is generally considered one of the last great European Romantic poems.

Eminescu's Romanian was at once the most archaic and the most natural of any poet of his time. He took words from the church Slavonic of the liturgy, from the Maramureș and Transylvanian dialects, from the philosophical vocabulary of German, and from the lexicon of Romanian folklore, and out of them he built a literary language that has the texture of speech. He died in a Bucharest psychiatric hospital in 1889, at thirty-nine. Within twenty years his face was on the banknote, and his name was given to a national cultural institute.

*Image caption: Mihai Eminescu, photograph by Jean Bielig, Bucharest, c. 1883. The standard posthumous portrait.*

### The press and the public

The art and the poetry needed a public, and the public was built by the press. **Ion Heliade Rădulescu** had founded the *Curierul Românesc* in 1829. **George Bariț** founded the *Gazeta de Transilvania* in Brașov in 1838, the first Romanian newspaper in the Habsburg Empire and a key vector for the union with Transylvania. **Timpul** (1876), the organ of the Conservative Party, was the paper for which Eminescu served as editor in his last decade, and in which his political articles appeared.

The **Junimea** journal *Convorbiri literare*, founded in 1867, published Eminescu, **Ion Creangă** (the great Romanian storyteller, author of *Amintiri din copilărie* — *Memories of My Youth*, 1881), **Ion Slavici**, and **Iacob Negruzzi**, with the support of the philologist **Titu Maiorescu**. The same years saw the *Romanian Athenaeum Society* running its lottery to build the concert hall on Calea Victoriei, and a parallel network of literary reviews producing the books that the new bookshops and libraries were buying. A language with a press, a poetry, a school of painting, a national theatre, and an opera house in progress was no longer a peasant dialect. It was a public medium.

### Enescu, 1881–1955

The last of the figures in this story is also the one who closes it, in a sense. **George Enescu** (1881–1955) is the only Romanian composer whose name most educated Europeans know.

He entered the **Vienna Conservatory** in **1888**, at seven, the youngest student ever admitted. He graduated in 1893, spent another year in **Robert Fuchs**'s composition class, and moved to the **Paris Conservatory** in **1895**. He studied violin with **Martin Pierre Marsick**, harmony with **André Gedalge**, and composition with **Jules Massenet** and **Gabriel Fauré**. His fellow students at the Conservatoire were **Maurice Ravel**, **Alfredo Casella**, **Alfred Cortot**, and **Jacques Thibaud**. He won the first prize for violin in **1899**.

What he brought back, after the long formation in Vienna and Paris, was the full European technique — symphonic structure, late-Romantic chromatic harmony, a French clarity of orchestration. His *Poème roumain* (1898), his *Romanian Rhapsodies* (1901–02), and his opera *Oedipe* (1936) were written for the same European stages that had already learned him. He refused to live under communism and lived in Paris from 1946 until his death in 1955.

His early life is the perfect emblem of the arc this annex has been tracing. A Romanian child admitted to the Vienna Conservatory at seven; a violinist in Paris at fourteen; a fellow student of Ravel; the technique brought home. The road the 1848 students had walked in the opposite direction — west to learn, east to make — was now a road travelled in both directions.

*Image caption: George Enescu, c. 1898. The Bucharest concert hall on Calea Victoriei has been the home of the George Enescu International Festival since 1958.*

### A circuit of forms

What the period 1830–1918 built was a small but functioning circuit of forms. The boyar and the bourgeois could go to the theatre (*Teatrul Național* reconstituted in 1852; the Romanian Opera opened in 1921 but was already the goal of the period); they could read the newspaper; they could buy the book; they could visit the National Salon (open since 1864 alongside Aman's school); they could subscribe to the *Convorbiri literare*; they could attend a concert at the Athenaeum; they could walk down a Calea Victoriei lined with Beaux-Arts palaces with the *Ateneu* at its head. The painter who had been trained in Paris was exhibiting in Bucharest. The poet who had read Schopenhauer in German was writing in the Romanian of his childhood. The violinist who had studied with Massenet and Fauré was composing in a Romanian idiom.

This is what a Western public culture looked like in the country that had not been a Western state for a generation and a half. It was not a copy. It was a circuit of forms, set up in a country that until very recently had been at the edge of three empires, and it worked because the same few hundred young people kept walking between Bucharest and Paris for forty years, until the forms had a home here too.

### Statement

> Art does not decorate freedom. It teaches freedom to be seen.
> *L'art ne décore pas la liberté. Il lui apprend à être vue.*

## 3. Editorial guidelines (for the builder)

These rules should govern the build, regardless of which model assembles the page. They are written so they can be lifted verbatim and dropped into a system prompt.

### Voice

- **One voice, not several.** The page is written in one literary register — short, declarative, image-led, French-trained. It does not switch to a guidebook voice for the dates or a hagiographic voice for the portraits.
- **Each portrait has a human shape.** The portraits are not CVs. They are short biographical passages that name a place (usually Paris, Vienna, or Berlin), a teacher, and a thing they brought back. The pattern is portable: *went to X, studied with Y, returned with Z*.
- **Quotations only when they are in the historical record.** "Dați un leu pentru Ateneu" is quotable because the slogan is in the historical record. "Disciple of the French school" is quotable because it is the Barbizon Museum's later characterisation. *"L'art ne décore pas la liberté"* is the page's own sentence, not a quotation.
- **No "X is not A. It is B" reframes.** This is the pattern that reads as AI-generated. Use a positive verb instead: "He was twenty years too early to see Romanian independence" reads better than "He did not live to see Romanian independence."

### Length

- **Hero deck: 1–2 sentences, 25–40 words.** A promise of the page, not a summary.
- **Portrait body: 4–6 sentences, 120–200 words.** A single tight paragraph per portrait. Do not split a portrait across two paragraphs.
- **Portrait headline: name + year, 2–5 words.** "Rosenthal, 1850" works; "The allegory of the nation" does not, on its own — it is the French echo.
- **Statement line: 1 short sentence.** A single image of the page's argument.

### Image rules

- **One hero image.** Use the existing `romania-revolutionary.jpg`. The deck sets the historical period.
- **One image per portrait.** Each named figure (Rosenthal, Aman, Grigorescu, Alecsandri, Eminescu, Enescu) gets a single image in their portrait section. If the image is a self-portrait or a known photograph, use it. If it is a representative painting, use the painting.
- **No decorative or generated imagery.** All images must be sourced from Wikimedia Commons, the named museums, or the existing asset folder. The Grigorescu landscape should be a real Grigorescu work, not a generic peasant image.
- **Captions are dated, credited, and short.** "*România revoluționară* · Constantin Daniel Rosenthal · c. 1850 · National Museum of Art of Romania."

### French echo

- **One French line per section headline, italic, restrained.** It is a translation of the headline, not of the body. It sits beneath the English headline, not after every paragraph.
- **No French echo for body sentences.** The body is English-first. The French is a resonance, not a duplicate.
- **The statement line is a single French sentence.** It is the only place on the page where the French is allowed to be a full clause, because the line is short by definition.

### Sourcing

- **Every named person has a source in the drawer.** No exceptions.
- **No quotations unless they have a citation.** The *Luceafărul* translations ("The Morning Star," "The Evening Star," "The Lucifer," "The Vesper") are listed because the multiple titles are in the academic record. Lovinescu's and Noica's glosses are quoted because both critics are in the canonical Eminescu scholarship.
- **Błażej Brzostek and the ICR essays are the preferred sources for the cultural-history framing.** Wikipedia is the entry point, not the final source, for the biographical details.

### Pitfalls to avoid

- **Do not turn the portraits into a list of names.** Six portraits at the right density is a page. Twelve names is a list. The list belongs in the calendar at the end, not in the body.
- **Do not treat Paris as a destination rather than a circuit.** The page is not "Romanians who went to Paris." It is "Romanians who walked a path between Bucharest and Paris for forty years." The grammar of the page should be reciprocal — Bucharest, Paris, Bucharest — not one-way.
- **Do not over-quote Eminescu.** *Luceafărul* is the most quoted poem in the Romanian canon; quoting it in translation flattens it. The page should let the prose carry the case and the title carry the poem.
- **Do not introduce the communist period as a redemption or a betrayal.** The page is about the formation of a public culture, not about its later political fate. The 1947–1989 period belongs, if at all, in a single sentence, and probably not in this page.
- **Do not call any of the six portraits "the most important."** They are different things — a symbol painter, a school founder, a landscape painter, a poet-diplomat, a metaphysical poet, a composer — and the page's argument is that the public culture is plural. Editorialising about which one matters most is the wrong move.

## 4. My thinking (for the author only)

The page is organised around a small number of **named individuals**, not around schools or movements. The argument is that a public culture is a list of people, not a list of institutions.

- **Why six portraits, not four or eight?** Four is too few to feel like a generation; eight is too many to remember. Six fits the page's reading time and gives the gender, class, and discipline the necessary variety: a symbol painter (Rosenthal), a school founder (Aman), a landscape painter (Grigorescu), a poet-diplomat (Alecsandri), a metaphysical poet (Eminescu), a composer (Enescu). The composers and poets of the *interwar* period (Enescu's pupils, the next generation of poets) are deliberately not on the page; the page is about the 1830–1918 formation, not the 1920–1940 maturity.
- **Why is Rosenthal first?** Because the painting is the page's most reproducible image and the 1848 generation is the political hinge. A page about Romanian art and poetry that did not open with the 1848 generation would feel politically evasive.
- **Why is Eminescu large and central?** Because Eminescu is the Romanian poet in the way that Pushkin is the Russian poet or Petrarch is the Italian poet. A page about Romanian poetry that gave Eminescu a small panel would read as either ignorant or irreverent. The Eminescu panel is allowed to be the longest, but it is not allowed to be a quotation dump.
- **Why is Grigorescu given two images and the longest panel?** Because the *plein-air* training in Paris and the *plein-air* work in Romania are two different things, and the page needs to make the distinction visible. The Napoleon III anecdote about *Apple Blossoms* is the rare verifiable fact that lets a non-specialist see Grigorescu as a real presence in the European art world of his time, not just a national figure.
- **Why a section on the press?** Because the portraits alone do not explain how the art and the poetry reached an audience. The press — *Curierul Românesc*, *Gazeta de Transilvania*, *Convorbiri literare*, *Timpul* — is the bridge between the named figures and the public. Without it, the page would read as a story of six individuals; with it, the page reads as a story of a public culture.
- **Why does Enescu close the page?** Because Enescu's biography is the reverse of the 1848 generation. The students of 1848 walked west to learn; Enescu walked west to learn and then walked home as a master. The closing line of the page — "the technique brought home" — is a reversal of the opening line of the main story ("students carry the idea east"). The two pages are meant to rhyme.
- **What I am not sure about.** I am not sure whether Alecsandri deserves a full section or should be folded into the press section. He is a major figure, but he is also the figure whose voice is the most "of his time," and a modern Western reader may not recognise the stakes. My instinct is to keep him as a portrait — he is the entry point for the folk material that Grigorescu and Eminescu are also working in — but a second pass may compress him.
- **What I am deliberately omitting.** No mention of Brâncovenesc painting, of the post-Byzantine icons, of the mural tradition. The page is about the *public* art that the 1848 generation begins, and the icon tradition is the pre-public ground the page starts from. A reader who wants the pre-history can find it in the long-form editing copy.
- **What I am deliberately not adjudicating.** Whether Eminescu's philosophical poetry is "more important" than Alecsandri's folk work, or whether Grigorescu's landscapes are "more national" than Rosenthal's allegory. The page presents each on its own terms and lets the reader decide.
