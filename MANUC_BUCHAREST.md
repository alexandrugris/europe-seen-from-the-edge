## A capital of strangers

Imagine a city of about eighty thousand people, where more than half of them were born somewhere else. Greek in the coffee houses, Armenian in the jewellery shops, German on the lips of Saxon craftsmen, Bulgarian in the cloth warehouses, French at the prince's table, and Romanian — at last given the dignity of a public language — on the street.

This is Bucharest in 1808, the year an Armenian merchant named Manuc Bey finishes his new inn. The French Revolution has been over for fifteen years. Napoleon is on the throne of Europe. The Russian and Ottoman Empires are fighting a war that will run through the city's streets. And a boy born in Ruse, on the Danube, is about to give Bucharest its most international courtyard.

## The man

Manuc's baptismal name is **Emanuel Mârzaian** (Armenian: Մանուկ Մարտիրոսի Միրզայան). He is Armenian by both parents, from the Ararat region. Born in 1769 in Ruse — then the Ottoman port of Rustchuk, on the Lower Danube — he arrives in the Moldavian capital Iași at the age of twelve, in the service of an Armenian merchant. He learns the languages the trade requires: Romanian first, then French, then Russian. Within a few years contemporaries will say he is fluent in about a dozen tongues.

He is, in other words, exactly the kind of figure the new century produces. A merchant, a banker, a diplomat, a political fixer — sometimes all four in the same morning. By 1803 the prince of Wallachia, Constantine Ypsilantis, has made him a boyar of the rank of *paharnic* (cupbearer). By 1807 the Sublime Porte in Constantinople has made him **Grand Dragoman** of the Ottoman Empire — chief interpreter, a kind of deputy foreign minister — the first time the post goes to a non-Phanariot. In 1808 Sultan Mahmud II grants him the title of **Bey of Moldavia**. (It is mostly honorary; he never rules the principality.)

His wealth is real. Contemporaries call him the richest man in the Balkans. He lends the Wallachian treasury **160,000 thalers**. He owns estates in the mountains near Predeal, where he tries a version of "modern agriculture" on his own lands, and properties across the Prut in what will soon be called Bessarabia. He is rumoured to be a Russian agent. He is certainly a Russian correspondent. He keeps a private line to Istanbul. He is, in a phrase one Romanian historian uses, a *secret actor* at the very centre of public events.

## A city of arrivals

Bucharest is the meeting point of the trade routes that run from Leipzig to Constantinople, from Vienna to the Black Sea, from Transylvania to the Levant. Its streets take their names from the trades and the merchants who keep them.

- **Lipscani** — the row of the *lipscani*, the Romanian merchants who buy cloth and metal goods at the great Leipzig fairs.
- **Gabroveni** — the cloth merchants from the Bulgarian market town of Gabrovo.
- **Șelari** — the saddlers; **Covaci**, the blacksmiths; **Blănari**, the furriers; **Șepcari**, the cap makers.
- **Zarafi** — the money-changers, who keep the city's foreign currency in order.

The Armenian community has been in the city for at least three centuries by 1808. By 1810 their numbers in Wallachia are estimated at around ten thousand. They are jewellers, coffee merchants, money-changers, dragomans. There is also a sizeable Greek colony — doctors, teachers, financiers, diplomats, the residue of a century of Phanariot rule (1711–1821) under which princes from Phanar, the Greek quarter of Constantinople, governed the principalities in the Sultan's name. There are Jews — Sephardi from the south, Ashkenazi from the north, the latter growing in numbers — and a long-established community of Bulgarian, Serbian, and Macedonian craftsmen. There are Germans, mostly Saxon and Transylvanian, in the workshops. There is even, by the 1830s, a small Russian and French presence, alongside a transient population of ten to twenty thousand people passing through.

The numbers in a 1830 census — taken a generation after Manuc's inn is finished, in the same political atmosphere — give a rough shape to the older city Manuc grew up in. Bucharest has **70,000 inhabitants**. **1,795 of them are foreign subjects**: 1,226 Austrian, 236 Russian, 158 Prussian, 94 British, 80 French. There are **2,301 Jews** in the count. Another 10,000–20,000 people are in transit through Wallachia. A later pre-statistical record, the *catagrafie* of 1838, fixes the city at **63,644** inhabitants.

## The costume, the language, the room

The visitor's eye is caught first by the clothes. Around 1820 the British painter **Sir Robert Ker Porter**, attending a ball in Bucharest, is impressed and amused at the same time:

> "The general costume was Turkish, and of every-coloured brocade, embroidered and be-furred; so far all was well, till the huge Valachian cap turned the whole to the ridiculous."

A few years later the clergyman **Robert Walsh**, after a long stay in Constantinople, sums the city up:

> "Bucharest, a modern city, a Paris of European Turkey... the meeting place of oriental and European manners. Half the inhabitants wear top hats and frock coats, the other half kalpaks and kaftans."

The boyars — the old landed aristocracy, increasingly mixed with Phanariot Greek and Aromanian families — are the most striking. Their costume is an Ottomanised court dress: the long *anteriu* buttoned to the waist, the *fermenea* (a fur-bordered waistcoat of gold-embroidered velvet), the heavy *caftan*, the *djubea* with its sable collar, the wide red *shalvars*, the soft *meshi* boots, and the tall *ishlik* — a cap so bulbous it can be read across a room. When the same boyars want to look modern, they borrow a "German" suit. French and German are already the two European languages a Wallachian gentleman is expected to read.

Language is fluid. Educated Romanians speak French. Walsh notes that among cultivated Romanians *"French is spoken better than in Brussels."* But the city is multilingual in a deeper way: the *lăutari* — the guild of Romani and Romanian musicians — sing in Romanian; the Greek clergy chant in Greek; the Armenians keep their church in Armenian; the Jews speak Ladino, Yiddish, or both; the Germans of the Saxon workshops speak their own dialect. A Greek musician named **Dionisie Fotino**, who settles in Bucharest around 1800, plays the *tambur* and the *keman* alongside the piano.

By 1817 Bucharest has its first theatre, founded at the request of *Ralu Caragea*, the youngest daughter of the Phanariot prince Ioan Caragea. By 1828 the Italian impresario **Momolo** has built another on the *Podul Mogoșoaiei* — the long, tree-lined avenue that becomes Calea Victoriei. Music, theatre, the new boulevard, the coffee house: a Western surface is being laid over an Ottoman city, and the seams are visible everywhere.

## A continent at the door

The Europe Manuc's courtyard is built into is a Europe in flames. Bucharest in 1808 is closer to the battlefields of Austerlitz, Jena, and Tilsit than the maps usually suggest. The war of 1806–1812 between Russia and the Ottoman Empire — the war that will end with the treaty signed in his inn — is itself a side theatre of the Napoleonic wars, and Bucharest is a listening post for all of them.

The spark is French. In the autumn of 1806 Napoleon sends **General Horace-François Bastien Sébastiani** to Constantinople as ambassador. Sébastiani's instructions are blunt: pressure the Sultan to dismiss the two pro-Russian Phanariot princes — **Constantine Ypsilantis** in Wallachia and **Alexander Mourousis** in Moldavia — in violation of Russia's protective rights over the principalities, obtained at Constantinople in 1802. The Sultan complies. Tsar Alexander I responds by sending sixty thousand troops across the Prut, under the command first of General Kamensky and then of **Mikhail Kutuzov**. By December 1806 Russian forces under **General Ivan Michelson** are in Bucharest. The war is on.

Behind that war, larger calculations. In the summer of 1807 Napoleon and the Tsar meet on a raft in the middle of the Niemen at **Tilsit**. They talk about the partition of the Ottoman Empire. Napoleon is willing to concede both Romanian principalities to Russia — but Constantinople, the prize of prizes, he keeps for himself. A year later, at **Erfurt** (1808), the conversation continues. Tilsit's hidden article 12 is later invoked to justify the cession of Bessarabia. The two emperors part, polite but wary. Napoleon is already planning the invasion of Russia that will begin in June 1812; the Tsar is already looking for a quiet southern front. Manuc's courtyard is, in a real sense, the room where that front is closed.

In Bucharest the wars arrive as a strange compound. The Russian officers who occupy the city from 1806 to 1812 are, as often as not, **French- and German-speaking men of French or German descent** — Petersburg aristocrats who grew up with Voltaire, who quote Racine, who ask the boyars' daughters to waltz. *"Petersburgian, that is, 'French' customs were instilled in the occupied city by the Russian army officers, many of whom were of French or German descent. They spoke French, wore their uniforms, and asked the local boyars' daughters for a waltz or polka."* The city's old Francophilia — already strong, since French has been taught at the Princely Academy since the 1770s — finds a new, embodied form. The Russian commander and the French ambassador's niece sit at the same dinner.

And the larger revolutionary vocabulary has been travelling east for twenty years. The 1791 *Supplex Libellus Valachorum* — the petition that asked the Habsburg emperor to recognise Romanians as a fourth nation in Transylvania — is openly inspired by French civic ideas, even though it is filed in Habsburg Vienna. Greek translations of the French revolutionary texts circulate in the principalities, smuggled through Constantinople, where Greek and Aromanian readers can pass them to Wallachian contacts. The Phanariot princes themselves, who govern the country in the Sultan's name, are products of the same Mediterranean world that produced the French Enlightenment: doctors, lawyers, diplomats, men of two or three languages. Some of them — most famously **Alexander Ypsilantis** and his son **Constantine**, who will lead the Greek revolution of 1821 — are the kind of people who keep a copy of *Les droits de l'homme* on the desk and a copy of the Gospels on the night table.

In the early 1820s the future Romanian intellectual elite is formed in this same atmosphere. The boyar families that send their sons to Paris do not send them to learn a foreign language. They send them to learn a foreign *civilisation*, and to bring it back. **Ion Heliade Rădulescu**, the editor of the first Romanian-language newspaper, will be fined for printing foreign news taken from the proscribed *Gazeta de Transilvania* instead of the censored Russian papers the authorities wanted. **Nicolae Bălcescu** and the Brătianu brothers will walk the same lecture halls of Paris that the Brătianu and Ypsilantis of 1808 walked. The students of 1848 read the news of February 1848 in Bucharest, the same way the officers of 1806 read the news of Austerlitz — from the Continent into the Black Sea, through French, into Romanian.

Manuc's courtyard is a small piece of that larger traffic. The treaty signed there on 28 May 1812 frees sixty thousand Russian troops to march north, against Napoleon. **General Langeron**, one of Kutuzov's officers, will later write that the Bucharest peace *"made a difference at the battle of Borodino."* Thirteen days after Manuc's guests put down their pens, Napoleon's Grande Armée crosses the Niemen. The treaty that two empires sign in a merchant's inn becomes a small but real gear in the largest military operation in European history. And it is the gateway through which Bessarabia leaves Moldavia and Russia enters the Lower Danube as a permanent presence.

## The courtyard

The plot Manuc buys lies on what is now **Strada Franceză** (it has been renamed several times — *Carol*, *30 Decembrie*, *Iuliu Maniu*), across the street from the ruins of the **Old Princely Court, *Curtea Veche***, the residence of Vlad Țepeș and his successors. He builds a *han* in the Ottoman-Balkan caravanserai tradition: rooms on two storeys looking onto a covered wooden gallery, supported on heavy oak columns, around a generous courtyard. It is built for movement — merchants, horses, goods, news, officials pass through — and for negotiation, since a courtyard is also a meeting place.

By the middle of the 19th century the inn is Bucharest's largest commercial complex: **fifteen wholesalers, twenty-three retail stores, 107 rooms for offices or living, two receiving halls, and a pub**. Manuc is no longer alive to see it — he dies in an accident in 1817 and is buried in the Armenian church in Chișinău, the new Russian city across the Prut that did not exist when he was born — but the inn keeps his name and keeps his trade.

## The war, the room, the treaty

From 1806 to 1812, Russian forces occupy Wallachia to keep the principalities out of French reach. Bucharest is, in effect, a Russian rear base. The general **Mikhail Miloradovich** checks a major Ottoman offensive at Obilești in June 1807. The older general **Mikhail Kutuzov** arrives in 1811 and destroys the Ottoman army at Slobozia. Through all of this, Manuc's courtyard does its work: the Russian and Ottoman officers who come to Bucharest pass through it; the meetings, the dinners, the introductions happen in it. In 1809 Manuc himself mediates between Miloradovich and a rebel Ottoman garrison at Giurgiu. Two years later, the room is given a much larger job.

In the winter of 1811 the Russo-Ottoman peace negotiations move from Giurgiu to Bucharest. The Russian plenipotentiaries are **Andrei Italinski**, **Ivan Sabaneev**, and **Iosif Fonton** — Fonton a Greek-speaking former consul at the Ottoman court. The Ottoman plenipotentiaries are **Galib efendi**, **Mehmed Said efendi**, and **Dimitrie Moruzi**, a Phanariot dragoman who speaks Russian and French. Behind them sit Kutuzov, who has just won the war, and the captured **Ahmed Pasha**.

For five months the two sides meet under Manuc's roof. On **3/15 May 1812**, after a long private meeting between Kutuzov and Galib efendi, they agree in principle. The treaty is signed on **16/28 May 1812** in the same courtyard.

The terms: Russia keeps the eastern half of Moldavia — the territory between the Prut and the Dniester, in Ottoman hands since 1484, soon to be renamed **Bessarabia**. The Ottomans keep Wallachia and the rest of Moldavia, under their old suzerainty. Russia gains trading rights on the Danube. The Serbs are granted autonomy.

Thirteen days later, Napoleon's Grande Armée crosses the Niemen into Russia. The 1812 settlement — drawn up in a merchant's courtyard, between two empires, in a city of eighty thousand people — becomes, almost by accident, the longest-lasting of the territorial changes the Napoleonic wars will produce in this part of Europe.

For Romania, the treaty is the beginning of a long argument. The principalities are technically returned to their old masters, but the experience cannot be undone. They have been administered by Russians. They have watched Greek Phanariot rule end in the revolution of Tudor Vladimirescu (1821). They have seen a French political vocabulary carried home by students from Paris, Berlin, and Vienna. The treaty of 1812 will be revised in 1856 (when southern Bessarabia returns to Moldavia), and again in 1878 (when Romania wins independence but loses southern Bessarabia back), and again in 1918, and in 1940. The single document Manuc hosts in his courtyard sets a clock that will run for more than a century.

## What remains

After Manuc's death the inn changes hands. In 1874 a new owner, Vasilescu Lambru, repairs it and renames it the **Grand Hôtel de la Dacie**. Around 1880 its hall becomes a theatre, and Bucharest's first Romanian operetta is performed there. In 1914–1916 the same hall is the meeting place of the Romanian "Greater Romania" party — speakers include Nicolae Filipescu, Take Ionescu, Barbu Ștefănescu Delavrancea, Octavian Goga — arguing for the union with Transylvania and Bukovina that will be declared at Alba Iulia on 1 December 1918. The building is nationalised in 1949. The **Cantacuzino** family, its later owners, recover it in 2007.

What is left of Manuc's Bucharest? The streets are still here: **Lipscani, Gabroveni, Franceză**. The Armenian church still stands. The Old Princely Court is still in ruins next door. The boyars are gone, and so are the kalpaks and the caftans. The treaty has been replaced by others, the empires by successor states, and Bessarabia by a complicated map of the twentieth century. But the courtyard is the same shape, and the wooden galleries still ring it, and the oak columns are still carrying the second-floor rooms where Manuc's guests slept.

In a city that has been rebuilt, rerouted, and renamed many times, that is not nothing.

## A small calendar

- **1769** — Emanuel Mârzaian born in Ruse (Ottoman Rustchuk).
- **c. 1781** — arrives in Iași; learns Romanian, French, Russian.
- **1803** — made *paharnic* boyar by Constantine Ypsilantis.
- **1807** — appointed Grand Dragoman of the Sublime Porte (first non-Phanariot).
- **1808** — builds the inn; granted the title of Bey of Moldavia.
- **1809** — mediates between Miloradovich and an Ottoman garrison at Giurgiu.
- **1811–1812** — hosts the Russo-Ottoman peace negotiations.
- **28 May 1812** — the Treaty of Bucharest is signed in his courtyard.
- **1817** — dies in an accident; buried in the Armenian church in Chișinău.

## A small atlas of names

- **Emanuel Mârzaian** — Manuc's baptismal name (Romanian; also Mîrzaian, Mirzaianț).
- **Manuc Bey** — the Turkish-Armenian form of his name, the one his contemporaries used.
- **Constantine Ypsilantis** — Phanariot prince of Wallachia (r. 1802–1806); Manuc's early patron.
- **Alexander Ypsilantis** — Constantine's son; leader of the Filikí Etería and the Greek uprising in Moldavia (1821); not himself a prince of the principalities, but the first to raise the standard of revolt on Ottoman-Orthodox ground.
- **Mikhail Miloradovich** — Russian general in Wallachia 1806–1809.
- **Ivan Michelson** — Russian general; enters Bucharest with the occupation force in December 1806.
- **Mikhail Kutuzov** — Russian commander-in-chief; chief Russian negotiator in 1811–1812.
- **Alexander I of Russia** — Tsar; meets Napoleon at Tilsit (1807) and Erfurt (1808); ratifies the Bucharest treaty in Vilna on 11/23 June 1812, the day before Napoleon crosses the Niemen.
- **Napoleon Bonaparte** — Emperor of the French; the absent presence in every Bucharest calculation of 1806–1812.
- **Horace-François-Bastien Sébastiani** — French ambassador to Constantinople; his dismissal of the pro-Russian Phanariot princes in 1806 is the immediate cause of the war.
- **Karađorđe Petrović** — Serbian revolutionary leader (1768–1817); the First Serbian Uprising (1804–1813) is the only other Christian revolt of the moment; the Bucharest treaty abandons it to the Ottomans, and Karađorđe eventually flees to Bessarabia.
- **Galib efendi** — Ottoman chief negotiator.
- **Andrei Italinski** — Russian plenipotentiary; former ambassador to Constantinople.
- **Dimitrie Moruzi** — Phanariot dragoman in the Ottoman delegation.
- **General Langeron** — Russian officer at Bucharest; later claims the 1812 treaty made a difference at Borodino.
- **Tudor Vladimirescu** — leader of the 1821 Wallachian uprising that ends Phanariot rule.
- **Ion Heliade Rădulescu** — editor of the first Romanian-language newspaper; fined for printing uncensored European news.
- **Nicolae Bălcescu, Ion and Dumitru Brătianu** — the next generation, in Paris from the 1840s on.
- **Robert Walsh** — British clergyman; his description of Bucharest is the most quoted.
- **Sir Robert Ker Porter** — British painter at the Bucharest ball of 1820.
- **Carol Popp de Szathmári** — Transylvanian Saxon painter and pioneer photographer, the first to picture the inn.
