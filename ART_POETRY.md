## Before the public

Until the 1820s the Romanian principalities have almost no public art and no public stage. Icons are painted in monasteries in the post-Byzantine tradition. Secular painting is a court craft. Theatre is in Greek — the language of the Phanariot princes — and is staged for boyar families in private rooms.

Two events change this. The first is the **end of Phanariot rule** in 1821, after Tudor Vladimirescu's uprising in Wallachia and the Greek revolution that follows. The principalities fall back to native boyar rule, and the local language begins to claim the public spaces. The second is the **1829 Treaty of Adrianople**, which opens the principalities to European trade and travel. Within ten years, the first Romanian-language newspaper (*Curierul Românesc*, edited by Ion Heliade Rădulescu, from 1829), the first Romanian-language school (*Gheorghe Lazăr* in Bucharest, 1818), and the first Romanian theatre (the *Theatre by the Red Drinking Fountain*, founded at the request of *Ralu Caragea* in 1817, the basis of the future National Theatre) are in place. The infrastructure of a public culture has begun.

The country that produces this first generation has very few artists. Almost every one of them has to be sent abroad, and the foreign capital that almost all of them will pass through is Paris.

## The 1848 generation: Rosenthal and the allegory of the nation

The **1848 revolution** in Wallachia is the political event that gives a generation of Romanian artists their first public subject. Among them is the painter **Constantin Daniel Rosenthal** (1820–1851), a Hungarian-born Transylvanian who has studied in Munich and Paris and has been living in Bucharest since 1847. He is at the side of the Rosetti family during the revolutionary days. He is among the painters who, after the revolution is crushed, walk into exile in Paris.

In Paris, in 1850, Rosenthal paints the small oil that is now the most reproduced image of the 1848 generation: ***România revoluționară*** — *Revolutionary Romania*. A woman in Romanian folk dress, bust-length, slightly turned to the left, holds the tricolour in her left hand and a dagger in her right. The model is **Maria Rosetti** (née Mary Grant, a Scot), the wife of the revolutionary **C. A. Rosetti** and one of the few women present at the barricades. The painter has been working from Delacroix — *Liberty Leading the People* and *Greece Expiring on the Ruins of Missolonghi* — and the painting's allegorical structure is the standard European Romantic one: an abstract political idea made into a female body, the nation's face as the face of a woman.

The painting is lithographed and circulated as propaganda among the Romanian exile community. In 1857 Rosenthal's heirs donate the original and a companion piece to the museum in Bucharest. Both paintings are now in the **National Museum of Art of Romania**. The image has become the standard visual shorthand for the 1848 generation — a generation that found in Paris not only the political vocabulary of 1848 but the visual vocabulary to give the new political idea a face.

Rosenthal dies of tuberculosis in 1851, in Paris, at thirty-one. He is twenty years too early to see Romanian independence.

## The first generation: Theodor Aman and the founding of the school

The painter who gives Romania its first generation of trained artists is **Theodor Aman** (1831–1891). Aman studies in Paris in the 1850s, returns to Bucharest in 1857, and spends the next seven years lobbying for a national art school. In **1864**, with his friend and colleague **Gheorghe Tattarescu** (1820–1894, trained in Rome), he persuades Prince Alexandru Ioan Cuza to sign the founding decree. The **National School of Fine Arts** in Bucharest — the ancestor of today's **Bucharest National University of Arts** — opens its doors. Aman is its first director, and he holds the post until his death in 1891.

The school is French in its curriculum, German in its discipline, and Romanian in its enrolment. The students — among them **Nicolae Grigorescu**, **Ștefan Luchian**, **Octav Băncilă**, and a generation of younger painters — will go on to define the visual canon of the modern country. None of them will work in a vacuum. Each of them will pass through a foreign capital — Paris for most, Munich for some — and each of them will come back to teach at Aman's school, or to be exhibited at the National Salon that opens in 1864 alongside the school itself.

## Grigorescu and the Barbizon

The most important of Aman's students is **Nicolae Grigorescu** (1838–1907), who is usually called the founder of modern Romanian painting. Grigorescu has been trained as an icon painter and has been painting frescoes in churches since childhood. In **1861** the Romanian state sends him to Paris on a scholarship. He enters the **École des Beaux-Arts** and is admitted to the workshop of **Sébastien Cornu**, where his fellow student is **Pierre-Auguste Renoir**. Within two years he has left the École and moved to **Barbizon**, the village south of Paris where the generation of **Théodore Rousseau**, **Jean-François Millet**, **Jean-Baptiste-Camille Corot**, and **Gustave Courbet** has just invented landscape painting in the open air.

Grigorescu stays in France, on and off, until 1869. He works the forest of Fontainebleau, paints the village of Marlotte, travels to Brittany (Vitré, Dinan) and Normandy. In 1867 he exhibits seven paintings at the **Paris Universal Exhibition**, including *Les Lavandières*. In **1868** he is one of a group of Barbizon painters whose work is shown to the imperial couple: **Napoleon III** and **Eugénie** buy his *Apple Blossoms* for the Tuileries collection. He returns to Bucharest in 1869, but he keeps a Paris studio until 1890. He is, the Barbizon Museum's later curator will write, *"the disciple of the French school"* who brought back to Bucharest a new way of seeing light.

What he brings is, more precisely, three things. The first is **plein-air painting** — the practice of working outside, in front of the subject, instead of finishing in the studio. The second is a **modern palette**, built around the broken greens, ochres, and pearly greys of the Barbizon painters, soon to be picked up by the Impressionists. The third is a way of painting **Romanian peasant life** — ox-carts on muddy roads, *ie* (farmhouse) courtyards with chickens, women washing clothes by the river, the same subjects Millet had used for the French peasant — as the legitimate subject of serious painting. The country is no longer the costume; it is the painting.

He paints the Romanian landscape of the 1870s and 1880s — the foothills near Câmpina, the villages of Muscel and Argeș — in thousands of small oil studies, and the country he gives Romania is the one his pupils will inherit.

## Alecsandri and the folk voice

In poetry, the equivalent of Grigorescu's peasant landscape is the work of **Vasile Alecsandri** (1821–1890): poet, playwright, folklorist, diplomat, minister. Alecsandri is the first major figure of modern Romanian literature. He publishes his first collection of folk poetry (*Poezii populare ale românilor*) in 1866; it includes *Despot Vodă* and *Dumbrava Roșie*, verse dramas based on Romanian medieval history. He writes lyrical poetry, comedies, and travel writing. He is Romania's foreign minister in 1859 and ambassador to Paris in 1880–1885.

Alecsandri's *Fântâna Blanduziei* (1843) is the first Romanian comedy; his *Despot Vodă* (1879) and *Fata de la Cozia* are the first Romanian historical dramas. He also collects folk songs, including the version of *Miorița* that becomes the Romanian national ballad. His position in the Romanian literary canon is what the position of a Béranger or a Lamartine is in the French: a poet who gives a young language the air of a literature.

The peasant-painting of Grigorescu and the folk-poetry of Alecsandri are doing the same cultural work from different sides: they are saying that the country's own past, in its own language, is fit material for a European public art. The next generation will not have to argue the case.

## Eminescu and the interior

The next generation's poet is **Mihai Eminescu** (1850–1889), and his case is different. Eminescu is a Romanian poet who reads **Schopenhauer** in German, **Kant** in German, and the **Upanishads** in German; he lives in Vienna (1869–72) and Berlin (1872–74); he is, in temperament, more German than French, and more metaphysical than political. He is not, on the surface, an inheritor of Alecsandri.

He is, however, a builder of a modern literary language. In the literary society **Junimea** in Iași — founded in 1863 by **Titu Maiorescu** and friends, with its journal *Convorbiri literare* — Eminescu publishes the poems that become the core of the modern canon: *Luceafărul* (1883), *Odă în metru antic*, the five *Scrisori* (Epistles). His Romanian is at once the most archaic and the most natural of any poet of his time: he takes words from the church Slavonic of the liturgy, from the Maramureș and Transylvanian dialects, from the philosophical vocabulary of German and from the lexicon of Romanian folklore, and out of them he builds a literary language that has the texture of speech.

*Luceafărul* — the title is variously translated as "The Morning Star," "The Evening Star," "The Lucifer," "The Vesper" — is his long philosophical poem, ten years in the making, published in 1883 from Vienna. It is based on a Romanian folk ballad but reaches into Indo-European myth and Schopenhauerian metaphysics; it is about genius and the impossibility of love; it is generally considered one of the last great European Romantic poems. The Romanian critic **Eugen Lovinescu** calls it *"suffused with Schopenhauerian pessimism."* The philosopher **Constantin Noica** reads it as a meditation on *"ontological dissatisfaction."* It is, in any case, the Romanian poem.

Eminescu dies in a Bucharest psychiatric hospital in 1889, at thirty-nine. He is mourned as a national poet. Within twenty years his face is on the banknote, and his name is given to a national cultural institute.

## The press: how a public reads

The art and the poetry need a public, and the public is built by the press. The **Curierul Românesc** (1829) is the first Romanian-language newspaper. **Gazeta de Transilvania** (1838), edited by **George Bariț** in Brașov, is the first Romanian newspaper in Habsburg territory and a key vector for the union with Transylvania. **Timpul** (1876), the organ of the Conservative Party, is the paper for which Eminescu serves as editor in his last decade and in which his political articles appear.

The press also has a literary role. The literary society **Junimea** publishes *Convorbiri literare* from 1867, with the support of the philologist **Titu Maiorescu** and a young team that includes Eminescu, **Ion Creangă** (the great Romanian storyteller, author of *Memories of My Youth*, 1881), **Ion Slavici**, and **Iacob Negruzzi**. Around the same time, the **Romanian Athenaeum Society** is running its lottery to build the concert hall on Calea Victoriei, and a parallel network of literary reviews is producing the books that the new bookshops and the new libraries are buying.

A language with a press, a poetry, a school of painting, a national theatre, and an opera house in progress is no longer a peasant dialect. It is a public medium.

## Enescu: the world comes back

The last of the figures in this story is also the one who closes it, in a sense. **George Enescu** (1881–1955) is the only Romanian composer whose name most educated Europeans know. He enters the **Vienna Conservatory** in 1888, at seven, the youngest student ever admitted. He graduates in 1893, spends another year in Robert Fuchs's composition class, and moves to the **Paris Conservatory** in 1895. He studies violin with **Martin Pierre Marsick**, harmony with **André Gedalge**, and composition with **Jules Massenet** and **Gabriel Fauré**. His fellow students at the Conservatoire are **Maurice Ravel**, **Alfredo Casella**, **Alfred Cortot**, and **Jacques Thibaud**. He wins the first prize for violin in 1899.

What he brings back, after the long formation in Vienna and Paris, is the full European technique — symphonic structure, late-Romantic chromatic harmony, a French clarity of orchestration. His *Poème roumain* (1898), his *Romanian Rhapsodies* (1901–2), and his opera *Oedipe* (1936) are written for the same European stages that have already learned him. He returns to Bucharest periodically, teaches, conducts, and refuses to live under communism; he lives in Paris from 1946 until his death in 1955.

His early life is the perfect emblem of the arc this annex has been tracing. A Romanian child is admitted to the Vienna Conservatory at seven; he is a violinist in Paris at fourteen; he studies with Massenet and Fauré; his first orchestral work is premiered in Paris; he is on the same benches as Ravel; he takes the technique home and gives Romania its first modern composer, and the Romanian Athenaeum is the natural seat of his festival. The road the 1848 students walked in the opposite direction — west to learn, east to make — is now a road travelled in both directions.

## A circuit of forms

What the period 1830–1918 has built is a small but functioning circuit of forms. The boyar and the bourgeois can go to the theatre (*Teatrul Național* is reconstituted in 1852; the Romanian Opera opens in 1921 but is already the goal of the period); they can read the newspaper; they can buy the book; they can visit the National Salon (open since 1864 alongside Aman's school); they can subscribe to the *Convorbiri literare*; they can attend a concert at the Athenaeum; they can walk down a Calea Victoriei lined with Beaux-Arts palaces and the *Ateneu* at its head. The painter who has been trained in Paris is exhibiting in Bucharest. The poet who has read Schopenhauer in German is writing in the Romanian of his childhood. The violinist who has studied with Massenet and Fauré is composing in a Romanian idiom.

This is what a Western public culture looks like in the country that has not been a Western state for a generation and a half. It is not a copy. It is a circuit of forms, set up in a country that until very recently was at the edge of three empires, and it works because the same few hundred young people keep walking between Bucharest and Paris for forty years, until the forms have a home here too.

- **1724** — Stavropoleos Church built in Bucharest; Brâncovenesc style, the visual inheritance of the pre-modern period.

- **1818** — Gheorghe Lazăr founds the first Romanian-language school in Bucharest.

- **1838** — *Gazeta de Transilvania*, the first Romanian newspaper in the Habsburg Empire, founded by George Bariț in Brașov.
- **1848** — Wallachian revolution; Rosenthal and the Rosettis in the barricades; Theodor Aman enters the Bucharest art world.
- **1850** — Constantin Daniel Rosenthal paints *România revoluționară* in Paris.
- **1851** — Death of Constantin Daniel Rosenthal in Paris, age 31.
- **1852** — The *Teatrul Național* is reconstituted in Bucharest.
- **1857** — Theodor Aman returns from Paris; begins the campaign for a national art school.
- **1861** — Nicolae Grigorescu arrives in Paris on a Romanian state scholarship; enters the École des Beaux-Arts.
- **1863** — Junimea literary society founded in Iași by Titu Maiorescu.

- **1867** — Grigorescu exhibits at the Paris Universal Exhibition.
- **1868** — Napoleon III and Eugénie buy Grigorescu's *Apple Blossoms* for the Tuileries.
- **1869** — Grigorescu returns to Bucharest; Junimea's *Convorbiri literare* begins publication.
- **1876** — *Timpul*, the Conservative newspaper, founded; Eminescu joins as editor.
- **1880–1885** — Vasile Alecsandri serves as Romania's ambassador in Paris.
- **1881** — Birth of George Enescu in Liveni, Moldavia.
- **1883** — *Luceafărul* published in Vienna.
- **1888** — Romanian Athenaeum inaugurated on Calea Victoriei; Enescu enters the Vienna Conservatory, age 7.
- **1889** — Death of Mihai Eminescu in Bucharest.
- **1891** — Death of Theodor Aman.
- **1895–1899** — Enescu at the Paris Conservatory; fellow students include Maurice Ravel.
- **1907** — Death of Nicolae Grigorescu in Câmpina.
- **1936** — Enescu's opera *Oedipe* premiered in Paris.
- **1955** — Death of George Enescu in Paris.
- **1958** — The George Enescu International Festival is established in Bucharest.

## A small atlas of names

- **Constantin Daniel Rosenthal** (1820–1851) — painter; *România revoluționară*, 1850; the visual emblem of the 1848 generation.
- **Maria Rosetti** (née Mary Grant, 1819–1903) — the model for Rosenthal's Romania; wife of C. A. Rosetti; one of the women of the 1848 barricades.
- **Theodor Aman** (1831–1891) — painter and engraver; founder of the National School of Fine Arts (1864).
- **Gheorghe Tattarescu** (1820–1894) — painter trained in Rome; co-founder of the school with Aman.
- **Nicolae Grigorescu** (1838–1907) — painter; École des Beaux-Arts and Barbizon; founder of modern Romanian painting.
- **Vasile Alecsandri** (1821–1890) — poet, playwright, diplomat; collector of Romanian folk poetry; ambassador to Paris.
- **Mihai Eminescu** (1850–1889) — poet; *Luceafărul* (1883); the modern Romanian literary language.
- **Titu Maiorescu** (1840–1917) — critic, philosopher, Junimea founder, and (later) prime minister; the first serious literary critic in Romanian.
- **Ion Heliade Rădulescu** (1802–1872) — the first editor of a Romanian-language newspaper (*Curierul Românesc*, 1829).
- **George Bariț** (1812–1893) — Transylvanian journalist; founder of *Gazeta de Transilvania* in Brașov, 1838.
- **George Enescu** (1881–1955) — composer, violinist, conductor, teacher; student of Massenet and Fauré; the first Romanian composer of European rank.
- **Ion Creangă** (1837–1889) — storyteller; *Memories of My Youth* (1881); a member of Junimea.
- **Ion Luca Caragiale** (1852–1912) — playwright and short-story writer; the comic master of late-19th-century Bucharest.
